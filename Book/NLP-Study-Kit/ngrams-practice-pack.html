Tough objective questions

Written at exam difficulty — several turn on a single word in the definition (e.g. "backoff" vs "interpolation", "type" vs "token"). Read every option before locking in an answer.

Q1Concept
A bigram model approximates P(wₙ | w₁…wₙ₋₁) as P(wₙ | wₙ₋₁). This approximation is formally called:
  • A. The chain rule
  • B. The Markov assumption
  • C. Maximum Likelihood Estimation
  • D. The independence assumption
The chain rule (A) is the exact decomposition that gets approximated. MLE (C) is how you estimate probabilities from counts, not the approximation itself. "Independence assumption" (D) is a trap — the words are NOT assumed independent; wₙ still depends on wₙ₋₁. The Markov assumption is specifically "predict the future from a limited, not infinite, history."
Q2Tricky
In the sentence "They picnicked by the pool, then lay back on the grass and looked at the stars." (punctuation excluded), what are the token and type counts respectively?
  • A. 14 tokens, 16 types
  • B. 16 tokens, 14 types
  • C. 16 tokens, 16 types
  • D. 14 tokens, 14 types
Tokens = total running words = 16. Types = distinct words = 14, since "the" occurs twice ("the pool", "the grass"/"the stars" — "the" appears three times actually, and "at"/"and" are each single) — the book gives this exact sentence with the answer 16 tokens / 14 types. Tokens ≥ types always, so any option with types > tokens (A) is impossible on its face.
Q3Numeric reasoning
A word occurs 400 times in a 1,000,000-word training corpus. Under Maximum Likelihood Estimation, which statement about P(word) = 0.0004 is most accurate?
  • A. It is the true probability of the word in all of English
  • B. It is the value that makes the observed training data most probable, not necessarily the best estimate for other corpora
  • C. It must be smoothed before it can be used at all
  • D. It is only valid if the corpus is balanced across genres
This is the book's own "Chinese" example. MLE, by definition, maximizes P(training data | model) — it says nothing about correctness in a different corpus/genre. (C) is false: unsmoothed MLE is usable, just fragile at zero/low counts. (D) confuses a design recommendation (use matched-genre training data) with a mathematical requirement.
Q4Tricky
Why does the bigram grammar in the mini-corpus example need an explicit end token </s> (not just a start token <s>)?
  • A. To make counting symmetric for readability only
  • B. Without it, probabilities for all sentences of a given length would sum to 1, making the whole language's probability infinite
  • C. </s> is required so P(wₙ|wₙ₋₁) can be computed at all
  • D. It prevents zero-probability bigrams
This is Chen & Goodman's (1998) point, quoted directly in the chapter footnote. (C) is wrong — bigram probabilities are computable without any boundary tokens. (D) is unrelated to smoothing/zero counts.
Q5Concept
A "held-out" set differs from a "development test set" mainly in that:
  • A. They are the same thing under different names
  • B. A held-out set is used to tune model parameters like interpolation λs; a devset is used to check performance during iterative development before the final untouched test set
  • C. A held-out set always comes from a different genre
  • D. A devset is only used once, while a held-out set is reused indefinitely
The chapter defines held-out data as extra data used to set parameters other than the raw N-gram counts (e.g., λ weights via EM). A devset exists because repeatedly evaluating on one "test" set implicitly tunes to it, so a fresh, truly-unseen final test set is still needed. They're related but distinct.
Q6Numeric reasoning
If a bigram language model assigns probability zero to one bigram in a test sentence, what happens to that sentence's perplexity?
  • A. It becomes exactly 1
  • B. It becomes negative
  • C. It becomes infinite (undefined)
  • D. It is unaffected, since perplexity only looks at unigrams
PP(W) = P(W)−1/N. If P(W)=0, this is 1/0 → ∞. This is precisely why smoothing is mandatory before perplexity can be computed on unseen test data — the chapter states this explicitly in §4.5.
Q7Concept
Perplexity can also be understood as:
  • A. The total number of N-grams in the training corpus
  • B. The weighted average branching factor of the language, per the model
  • C. The vocabulary size V
  • D. The number of parameters in the model
Directly from §4.4. The 10-equiprobable-digits case gives PP=10 exactly because the branching factor is 10; skewing the distribution (one digit far more frequent) lowers PP below 10 even though the branching factor is unchanged — because perplexity weights branches by probability.
Q8Tricky
Two language models are compared by perplexity on the same test set. Model A uses a 50k-word closed vocabulary; Model B is open-vocabulary with <UNK>. Which statement is correct?
  • A. Model B's perplexity is automatically lower because it handles unknown words
  • B. The comparison is only meaningful if both models use the same vocabulary, since closed-vocabulary knowledge can artificially deflate perplexity
  • C. Perplexity cannot be computed for open-vocabulary models
  • D. Vocabulary choice never affects perplexity
The chapter is explicit: "the perplexity of two language models is only comparable if they use the same vocabulary" — closed-vocabulary knowledge of the test set can artificially lower perplexity, so it must be applied equally to be a fair comparison.
Q9Concept
Why does Laplace (add-one) smoothing perform poorly for N-gram language modeling in practice?
  • A. It cannot assign any probability to unseen N-grams
  • B. With a large vocabulary V, it moves far too much probability mass away from frequently-seen N-grams toward unseen ones
  • C. It only works for unigrams, not bigrams
  • D. It requires a held-out set to function
The BeRP example shows C(want to) discounted from 608 to 238 (factor 0.39) and C(chinese food) from 82 to 8.2 (factor 0.10) — a factor-of-10 swing. That's the "sharp change... because too much probability mass is moved to all the zeros," quoted directly from §4.5.1.
Q10Numeric reasoning
In Good-Turing smoothing, if N₁ = 5,315 (number of singleton bigrams) and N = total bigram token count, the estimated total probability mass reserved for all unseen bigrams combined is:
  • A. N₁ × N
  • B. N₁ / N
  • C. N / N₁
  • D. 1 / N₁
Eq. 4.27: P*_GT(unseen) = N₁/N. Note this is one number for the entire unseen category — it then gets divided further among individual unseen N-grams (as in the fish example, where the mass 3/18 was split between catfish and bass).
Q11Concept
The Kneser-Ney "continuation probability" for a word w is proportional to:
  • A. The raw unigram count of w
  • B. The number of distinct word types that precede w in the corpus
  • C. The number of times w starts a sentence
  • D. The length of w in characters
This is the "San Francisco" vs. "glasses" argument: Francisco has high raw frequency but almost only follows San (few distinct preceding contexts), while glasses follows many different words. Kneser-Ney backs off using |{wᵢ₋₁ : C(wᵢ₋₁w)>0}|, not raw frequency.
Q12Tricky
In Katz backoff, why can't we simply use raw (undiscounted) MLE probabilities at each level and back off to the lower-order model whenever the higher-order count is zero?
  • A. Because lower-order models are always less accurate
  • B. Because the resulting distribution would no longer sum to 1 — extra probability mass would be double-counted
  • C. Because MLE probabilities can be negative
  • D. Because backoff only works with Good-Turing discounting, never with raw counts
Directly from §4.7: "without discounts and α weights, the result... would not be a true probability!" MLE probabilities already sum to 1 across each context; adding backed-off mass on top with no discount pushes the total above 1. Discounting frees up exactly the mass that backoff redistributes.
Q13Concept
What is the essential difference between backoff and interpolation?
  • A. Backoff uses Good-Turing; interpolation uses Laplace
  • B. Backoff uses only the highest-order N-gram with nonzero count; interpolation always mixes all orders together, weighted
  • C. Interpolation is only for trigrams; backoff is only for bigrams
  • D. They are mathematically identical, just different names
Backoff "only backs off to a lower-order N-gram if we have zero evidence for a higher-order N-gram" — otherwise it trusts the higher order completely. Interpolation "always mixes the probability estimates from all the N-gram estimators," even when the trigram count is healthy.
Q14Numeric reasoning
In context-conditioned interpolation, Σᵢ λᵢ(w) must equal:
  • A. V (vocabulary size)
  • B. N (corpus size)
  • C. 1, for every context
  • D. 0
Eq. 4.33: Σᵢλᵢ=1. This holds even when the λs are context-dependent functions λᵢ(wⁿ⁻¹ₙ₋₂) — for any fixed context, the weights on unigram+bigram+trigram must still sum to 1 or the result isn't a valid probability distribution.

Q16Concept
Class-based (IBM clustering) N-grams estimate P(wᵢ|wᵢ₋₁) as:

  • B. P(cᵢ|cᵢ₋₁) × P(wᵢ|cᵢ)

Q17Tricky
Cache language models mix a unigram model of the preceding document text with a static N-gram. Their known weakness is:
  • A. They cannot be combined with any other N-gram model
  • B. In speech recognition, if an earlier word was itself mis-recognized, the cache "locks in" that error and biases future predictions
  • C. They only work for unigrams, not for higher-order N-grams
  • D. They require a closed vocabulary
Stated directly in §4.9.4: cache models work well when previous words are known exactly (e.g., text prediction with user-corrected input) but poorly in ASR pipelines lacking correction, since a wrong earlier guess propagates forward via the cache.
Q18Numeric reasoning
Entropy H(X) = −Σ p(x)·log₂p(x) is minimized (equal to 0) when:
  • A. One outcome has probability 1 and all others have probability 0
  • B. All outcomes are equally likely
  • C. There are exactly 2 outcomes
  • D. The vocabulary is infinite
Zero entropy = zero uncertainty = a fully deterministic outcome. Option B is the opposite case — a uniform distribution over N outcomes gives the maximum possible entropy, log₂N, not the minimum.
Q19Concept
Cross-entropy H(p,m) relates to the true entropy H(p) of a language by:
  • A. H(p) ≤ H(p,m) always — cross-entropy is an upper bound, and a better model m makes the bound tighter
  • B. H(p,m) ≤ H(p) always
  • C. H(p,m) = H(p) for any model m
  • D. The two are unrelated
Eq. 4.63. This is exactly why perplexity is a valid proxy for model quality: since PP=2^H(W) is built from cross-entropy against an approximating model, and cross-entropy can never underestimate the truth, a lower perplexity model is a demonstrably closer approximation, never an accidentally-optimistic one.
Q20Tricky
Chomsky's (1956/1957) critique of "finite-state Markov processes" as a model of language most directly led to which historical outcome, per the chapter's bibliographic notes?
  • A. N-gram research was proven mathematically incorrect and abandoned permanently
  • B. Many linguists and computational linguists ignored statistical modeling for decades, even though N-grams later resurged through IBM and CMU speech recognition work
  • C. Markov chains were replaced entirely by context-free grammars in all applications
  • D. Shannon retracted his 1948 entropy work in response
The chapter is precise about this: Chomsky's arguments (about competence vs. a useful engineering heuristic) caused statistical modeling to be sidelined in linguistics for years, but N-grams resurged in the 1970s–80s via Jelinek/Mercer/Bahl at IBM and Baker at CMU for speech recognition — a sociological outcome, not a mathematical refutation.

End-of-chapter exercise solutions

All ten exercises from §4.12, solved or given a complete solution method. 4.2–4.7 are programming tasks — implementation approach and reference pseudocode are given rather than a single "answer."

4.1 — Write the trigram probability estimation equation

Modify Eq. 4.14 (the bigram MLE equation) to the trigram case.

P(wₙ | wₙ₋₂ wₙ₋₁) = C(wₙ₋₂ wₙ₋₁ wₙ) / C(wₙ₋₂ wₙ₋₁)

Same pattern as Eq. 4.15 generalized: numerator = count of the full N-gram, denominator = count of its (N−1)-word prefix. For the very first trigram of a sentence, use two boundary tokens: P(w₁|<s><s>).

4.2 — Program to compute unsmoothed unigrams and bigrams

Core algorithm (language-agnostic):

1. Tokenize each sentence; prepend <s>, append </s>. 2. unigram_counts[w] += 1 for every token w (including </s>, excluding <s>'s own count if you don't want to predict it). 3. bigram_counts[(w_prev, w)] += 1 for every adjacent pair. 4. P(w) = unigram_counts[w] / N 5. P(w | w_prev) = bigram_counts[(w_prev,w)] / unigram_counts[w_prev]
from collections import Counter, defaultdict

def train(sentences):
  uni, bi = Counter(), Counter()
  for s in sentences:
    toks = ["<s>"] + s + ["</s>"]
    uni.update(toks)
    bi.update(zip(toks, toks[1:]))
  return uni, bi

def p_bigram(w_prev, w, uni, bi):
  return bi[(w_prev, w)] / uni[w_prev]

4.3 — Compare statistics from two small corpora

Since this asks you to run your own program on your own data, the graded part is the comparison methodology, not one fixed numeric answer. What to report:

• Top-10 unigrams by frequency in each corpus — expect function words (the, to, i, a) to dominate both, since Zipf's law holds regardless of genre. • Where the two diverge: content words specific to register (e.g. "regards"/"attached" in email vs. slang/topic words in newsgroup posts). • Top bigrams: email tends to have fixed collocations ("please find", "let me", "thanks for"); newsgroups skew toward domain jargon and quoted-reply artifacts (">", "wrote:"). • Report V (types) and N (tokens) for each — the corpus with more informal/misspelled text usually has a larger V for the same N (Gale & Church's V ≳ O(√N) growth is genre-sensitive).

4.4 — Add random sentence generation

Implements the Shannon (1951) technique used to generate Fig. 4.3/4.4 in the chapter.

1. Start with context = ("<s>",) [or ("<s>","<s>") for trigram]. 2. Lay all candidate next-words on the interval [0,1], each occupying a slice proportional to P(w | context). 3. Draw r ~ Uniform(0,1); emit the word whose slice contains r. 4. Slide the context forward by one word; repeat until </s> is emitted.
import random
def generate(model, order=2):
  ctx = ("<s>",) * (order-1)
  out = []
  while True:
    words, probs = model.next_word_dist(ctx)
    w = random.choices(words, weights=probs)[0]
    if w == "</s>": break
    out.append(w); ctx = (ctx+(w,))[-(order-1):]
  return " ".join(out)

4.5 — Add Good-Turing discounting

1. Build the frequency-of-frequencies table: N꜀ = number of distinct N-grams occurring exactly c times. 2. (Simple Good-Turing) Smooth the N꜀ sequence via log-log linear regression: log(N꜀) = a + b·log(c), to avoid undefined c* when N꜀₊₁ = 0. 3. For c ≤ k (Katz suggests k=5): c* = (c+1)·N꜀₊₁/N꜀, using the smoothed N꜀ values. 4. For c > k: keep c* = c (large counts are already reliable). 5. Reserve P*(unseen) = N₁/N as the missing mass, per Eq. 4.27.

4.6 — Implement Katz backoff

1. Compute Good-Turing-discounted P* for every N-gram with count > 0 (from 4.5). 2. For each context, compute leftover mass β(context) = 1 − Σ P*(w|context) over seen continuations. 3. Compute the normalizer α(context) = β(context) / Σ P_katz(w|shorter context) over w unseen in the full context. 4. At query time: if C(context,w) > 0 → return P*(w|context); else → return α(context) × P_katz(w | context minus its oldest word), recursing down to the unigram base case.
P_katz(z|x,y) = P*(z|x,y)             if C(x,y,z) > 0
          = α(x,y)·P_katz(z|y)  else if C(x,y) > 0
          = P*(z)                   otherwise

4.7 — Compute the perplexity of a test set

1. Concatenate the test set's log-probabilities: logP(W) = Σᵢ log P(wᵢ | wᵢ₋₁) (bigram case), including </s> but not the leading <s> in the token count N. 2. H = −logP(W) / N  (cross-entropy per word). 3. PP(W) = 2^H  (if logs were base 2) or exp(H) if natural logs were used consistently.
import math
def perplexity(test_sents, model):
  logp, N = 0.0, 0
  for s in test_sents:
    toks = ["<s>"] + s + ["</s>"]
    for w_prev, w in zip(toks, toks[1:]):
      logp += math.log2(model.p(w, w_prev))
      N += 1
  return 2 ** (-logp / N)

4.8 — Prove Eq. 4.27 from Eq. 4.26 (Michael Collins' exercise)

Claim: given c* = (c+1)·N꜀₊₁/N꜀ (Eq. 4.26), show P*GT(unseen) = N₁/N (Eq. 4.27).

Step 1 — apply Eq. 4.26 at c=0: c*₀ = (0+1)·N₁/N₀ = N₁/N₀. Step 2 — total re-estimated count mass in the "unseen" bin: there are N₀ distinct unseen N-grams, each re-assigned the adjusted count c*₀. Total mass for the bin = N₀ × c*₀ = N₀ × (N₁/N₀) = N₁. Step 3 — convert count mass to probability: any adjusted count is turned into a probability by dividing by N, the total number of (training) tokens/observations: P*GT(unseen) = (N₀ × c*₀) / N = N₁ / N. ∎

Assumptions used: (i) all N꜀ are non-zero so Eq. 4.26 is defined, (ii) probabilities are obtained from adjusted counts the same way as from raw MLE counts — by dividing by N. Notice N₀ cancels algebraically, which is exactly why the (unknowable, astronomically large) count of unseen bigrams N₀ never actually needs to be computed. Sanity check: summing (c+1)N꜀₊₁ over all c from 0 upward telescopes to Σc·N꜀ = N, confirming Good-Turing redistributes the full probability mass consistently.

4.9 — Bag generation (advanced)

Given a bag of words (order destroyed), reconstruct the most probable ordering using an N-gram LM as the scoring function.

1. Brute-force is infeasible: n words have n! orderings. 2. Treat it as a shortest-path / DP problem over subsets, à la the Held–Karp TSP algorithm — this is the connection to Viterbi the exercise hints at. 3. State: (S, w) where S ⊆ bag is the set of words placed so far and w ∈ S is the last word placed. Value: best[S,w] = highest log-probability of any ordering of S ending in w. 4. Recurrence: best[S,w] = maxₓ∈S\{w} ( best[S\{w}, x] + log P(w | x) ), with base case best[{w},w] = log P(w | <s>). 5. Answer = maxᵥ best[full bag, v] + log P(</s> | v); backtrack pointers recover the actual order. 6. Cost: O(2ⁿ·n²) — exponential in n but tractable for short sentences (n ≤ ~15–20), which is why this is framed as a Viterbi-style dynamic-programming exercise rather than a greedy one.

4.10 — Authorship attribution via singleton-word Bayesian scoring

Method exactly as specified: build a unigram LM per candidate author, use only words that occur exactly once in the unknown text, and compare geometric means of their probabilities under each author model.

1. Train unigram LMA on Author A's known writings; LMB on Author B's. 2. Find every word in the disputed text T that occurs exactly once in T — its singleton set S(T). 3. P(T|A) = ( ∏_{w ∈ S(T)} PA(w) )^(1/|S(T)|) — the geometric mean, computed only from LMA's probabilities for those words (using <UNK> smoothing for words A never used). 4. Compute P(T|B) the same way from LMB. 5. Attribute T to whichever author gives the higher geometric mean.

Why singletons specifically: rare/idiosyncratic word choices (an author's "tics") are more discriminating than function-word frequencies, which are similar across most authors of the same language and era. See the worked toy numeric version of this exact method in the Numericals tab (Problem N9).

N1 — Bigram MLE from a raw count table

A tiny corpus yields these bigram and unigram counts:

thecatsatC(row)
the018040
cat201520
sat10012

Find P(cat | the) and P(sat | cat).

P(cat|the) = C(the cat)/C(the) = 18/40 = 0.45
P(sat|cat) = C(cat sat)/C(cat) = 15/20 = 0.75

Notice C(row) here isn't just the row's own bigram counts summed (there may be other continuations not shown) — always use the given unigram count C(wₙ₋₁), not a re-summed row total, unless you're told the table is exhaustive.

N2 — Perplexity of a short test sentence

A bigram model gives: P(i|<s>)=0.25, P(want|i)=0.32, P(a|want)=0.65, P(flight|a)=0.5, P(</s>|flight)=0.2. Compute the perplexity of "i want a flight" (N=5, counting </s> but not <s>).

P(W) = 0.25 × 0.32 × 0.65 × 0.5 × 0.2 = 0.0052
PP(W) = P(W)^(−1/5) = 0.0052^(−0.2)
ln(0.0052) = −5.259 → −5.259/5 = −1.0518 → PP = exp(1.0518)

PP(W) ≈ 2.86

A perplexity near 2.86 with 5 words means the model was, on average, about as uncertain as choosing among ~3 equally likely words at each step — a well-fitted in-domain model.

N3 — Add-one smoothing of a zero-count bigram

Vocabulary V = 500. The word "blue" occurs C(blue)=90 times. The bigram "blue sky" never occurs: C(blue sky)=0. Find the Laplace-smoothed P*(sky|blue).

P*(sky|blue) = [C(blue sky)+1] / [C(blue)+V] = (0+1)/(90+500) = 1/590

P*(sky|blue) ≈ 0.00169

Compare to what MLE alone would give: exactly 0, which would zero out any sentence containing "blue sky." Laplace trades that hard zero for a small but non-zero, uniformly-assigned probability.

N4 — Good-Turing on a birdwatching count (fish example, remixed)

Over a season, a birdwatcher logs 7 species: crow ×12, sparrow ×7, robin ×4, finch ×2, wren ×1, dove ×1, jay ×1 — out of 10 species in the local field guide (3 never seen: eagle, hawk, owl). Find (a) the probability the next sighting is an unseen species, (b) the probability it's specifically an eagle, and (c) the Good-Turing adjusted count for a singleton species like wren.

N = 12+7+4+2+1+1+1 = 28 total sightings. N₁ (species seen exactly once) = {wren, dove, jay} → N₁ = 3. N₂ (species seen exactly twice) = {finch} → N₂ = 1.
(a) P*GT(unseen) = N₁/N = 3/28 ≈ 0.107
(b) P*GT(eagle) = (1/3 unseen species) × 3/28 = 1/28 ≈ 0.0357
(c) c*(wren) = (1+1)·N₂/N₁ = 2×(1/3) = 0.667 (vs. raw MLE count of 1)

This mirrors the book's trout/catfish/bass example exactly: the singleton count of 1 gets discounted down to 0.667, and that "stolen" 0.333 of probability mass (×3 singleton species = 1 unit of count) is what funds the unseen-species estimate.

N5 — Entropy of a biased 4-outcome source

A source emits 4 symbols with P(A)=0.5, P(B)=0.25, P(C)=0.125, P(D)=0.125. Compute H(X) in bits, and compare to the entropy of a uniform 4-symbol source.

H(X) = −[0.5·log₂0.5 + 0.25·log₂0.25 + 0.125·log₂0.125 + 0.125·log₂0.125]
   = −[0.5×(−1) + 0.25×(−2) + 0.125×(−3) + 0.125×(−3)]
   = 0.5 + 0.5 + 0.375 + 0.375

H(X) = 1.75 bits  (uniform 4-symbol source: H = log₂4 = 2 bits)

Skewing the distribution always lowers entropy below the uniform maximum (log₂ of the outcome count) — exactly the same relationship exploited in Problem N6 below and in the chapter's digit-perplexity discussion.

N6 — The digit-perplexity calculation the book "leaves to the reader"

10 digits (0–9) are recognized. Digit "0" occurs 10× more often than each other digit (which are equally likely among themselves). Find the perplexity of this mini-language, and confirm it's lower than the uniform case's PP=10.

Let each non-zero digit have probability p; then P(0)=10p. Normalizing: 10p + 9p = 1 → p = 1/19. So P(0) = 10/19 ≈ 0.5263, and P(other digit) = 1/19 ≈ 0.0526 each. Entropy: H = −[P(0)log₂P(0) + 9·P(other)log₂P(other)] = −[0.5263×(−0.926) + 9×0.0526×(−4.248)] = −[−0.4875 − 2.0121] = 2.4996 ≈ 2.5 bits
Perplexity = 2^H = 2^2.5

PP ≈ 5.66  (down from PP=10 in the uniform case)

The branching factor is still literally 10 (ten digits are still possible), but perplexity — the weighted branching factor — drops because "0" is so predictable that most digits carry little surprise. This is precisely the intuition the chapter asks you to confirm by calculation.

N7 — P(i want chinese food) from the BeRP table (the book's other "left to the reader")

Using Fig. 4.2's probabilities — P(i|<s>)=0.25, P(want|i)=0.33, P(chinese|want)=0.0065, P(food|chinese)=0.52, P(</s>|food)=0.68 — compute P(<s> i want chinese food </s>) and compare it to the book's worked value for "i want english food" (0.000031).

P = 0.25 × 0.33 × 0.0065 × 0.52 × 0.68
= 0.0825 × 0.0065 × 0.52 × 0.68
= 0.00053625 × 0.52 × 0.68

P(i want chinese food) ≈ 0.00019 (1.9 × 10⁻⁴)

About 6× higher than "i want english food" (3.1 × 10⁻⁵) — mainly because P(chinese|want)=0.0065 dwarfs P(english|want)=0.0011, and P(food|chinese)=0.52 beats P(food|english)=0.5 only slightly. The chapter calls this a "cultural" fact baked into the bigram counts, not a syntactic one: nothing is grammatically wrong with either phrase.

N8 — Kneser-Ney continuation probability

In a corpus with 40,000 distinct bigram types total, the word "Francisco" follows only 1 distinct preceding word ("San"), while "cheap" follows 6 distinct preceding words (very/so/too/quite/get/find cheap). Compute P_CONTINUATION for each and state which word is more "portable" to new contexts.

P_CONT(Francisco) = |{w : C(w,Francisco)>0}| / Σ = 1/40,000 = 0.000025
P_CONT(cheap) = 6/40,000 = 0.00015

"cheap" is 6× more "continuation-worthy" than "Francisco", despite Francisco likely having a higher raw unigram count.

This is exactly why Kneser-Ney backs off to P_CONTINUATION instead of the raw unigram MLE — it correctly predicts that "cheap" is the safer bet after an unfamiliar adjective-modifier context, while "Francisco" is a one-trick word tied almost entirely to "San".

N9 — Toy worked example of Exercise 4.10 (authorship attribution)

Author A's 100-word training corpus has singleton words {hitherto, wherefore, verily} (each P=1/100=0.01 under LMA). Author B's 100-word corpus has singleton words {basically, actually, okay} (each P=0.01 under LMB, but "wherefore" and "verily" never appear in B's writing at all). A disputed text T has exactly two singleton words: "wherefore" and "verily". Which author does the geometric-mean test attribute T to?

P(T|A) = (PA(wherefore) × PA(verily))^(1/2) = (0.01 × 0.01)^0.5 = 0.01
P(T|B) = (PB(wherefore) × PB(verily))^(1/2) = (≈0 × ≈0)^0.5 ≈ 0  (unseen in B's corpus)

P(T|A) ≫ P(T|B) → the text is attributed to Author A.

This is a deliberately extreme toy case to make the mechanism visible: in a real run you'd apply <UNK>-style smoothing so B's probabilities aren't literally zero, but the geometric mean would still be far smaller than A's, since B's model has never encountered either word in any form. Real authorship studies (Mosteller & Wallace on the Federalist Papers) use richer feature sets, but the singleton-word Bayesian comparison is the simplified version this exercise asks you to implement.
