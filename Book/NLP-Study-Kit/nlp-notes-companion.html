27/07 – 28/07

What NLP is, and where it sits

Your definition

Natural language ≠ generated by a system

Your note is precise: natural language is spoken by human beings — written or spoken — as opposed to an artificial/formal language (like a regex or a programming language) that a system defines. NLP is the field that gets computers to handle that naturally-occurring, messy signal.

Disfluency

Filler words = disfluency

"hmm", "uh", "haan" — your note calls these pct phrases (parenthetical/filler phrases). The book's term is exactly this: disfluencies, split into fillers/filled pauses (uh, um) and fragments (broken-off words like "main-"). They're sometimes ungrammatical, but Stolcke & Shriberg (1996) found treating "uh" as a real word actually improves next-word prediction — because a disfluency itself is informative about what's coming.

Nesting diagram

AI ⊃ ML ⊃ DL, with NLP overlapping ML and DL

Your Venn diagram is the standard one: AI is the broadest goal (build systems that act intelligently); ML is the subset of AI that learns patterns from data instead of being hand-coded; DL is the subset of ML using multi-layer neural networks. NLP is not nested inside ML/DL — it's an application area (language) that increasingly uses ML/DL as its tools, which is why your diagram draws it overlapping rather than fully contained.

ML → learns a feature mapping from labeled examples (train / test split)
DL → learns features automatically via layered representations
Inference → the trained model's act of prediction on new input
Inductive bias → the built-in assumptions a model makes before seeing any data
Tokenizer, pretrained

Converts text → processable units, returns token-ids

This is the modern (subword-era) framing: a pretrained tokenizer doesn't just split on whitespace — it maps each piece to an integer id from a fixed vocabulary, which is what a neural model actually consumes. See Byte-Pair Encoding for how that vocabulary gets built.

Computational Linguistics (CL)

The scientific side; NLP is the engineering side

Your note: "CL → Analysis of language." The distinction that's usually drawn: CL asks what computational methods reveal about language (a linguistics question); NLP asks how to build systems that do something useful with language (an engineering question). In practice the two communities share almost all their tools.

LLM input modalities

Written text · spoken text/speech · visual (image/video)

Your bracket lists exactly the three input modalities a modern large model can be built to consume. "ASR output/transcript → text" is the bridge for the speech modality: an Automatic Speech Recognition system turns audio into a transcript, and everything downstream (tagging, parsing, understanding) runs on that transcript exactly as if it had been typed. This is also why the note "transcript → spoken text" appears right where it does — it's defining vocabulary before the Turing Test discussion that follows.

28/07 – 29/07

Turing, ELIZA, and machine translation's false start

Turing Test

An evaluator judges by transcript alone

Your note is correct: the evaluator can't see either party, only read the transcript, and must decide which side is human. It's a test of indistinguishability in conversation, not a test of "true" intelligence or understanding — a distinction worth having ready for an exam ("what does the Turing Test actually measure?").

ELIZA (1966)

Joseph Weizenbaum, MIT — the first well-known chatbot

ELIZA simulated a Rogerian (person-centered) psychotherapist. Textbook §2.1.6 shows exactly how: it's a cascade of regex substitutions. "my" → YOUR, "I'm" → YOU ARE, then pattern-triggered replies like s/.* all .*/IN WHAT WAY/. It has zero understanding — it reflects your sentence back using surface pattern matching, which is precisely why it's a perfect illustration of regex substitution with memory (the \1 register trick), not of language understanding.

Worth double-checking with your instructor: your note dates the first MT system to "1970's." The Georgetown–IBM experiment (the IBM 701 running a Russian→English demo, with a small ~250-word vocabulary and 6 grammar rules) is standardly dated to 1954, not the 1970s — it's usually cited as the opening shot of MT research, followed by the 1966 ALPAC report that cut MT funding for years. The "bilingual dictionary + grammar rules" two-part method your note lists (word-to-word translation, then structural rules) is exactly the rule-based MT paradigm that experiment used.
Grammar rules → POS → chunking → NER

The classical NLP pipeline, in your own worked example

Your Hindi→English example ("mein ghar gaya" → Pronoun-Noun-Verb → "I went home" → Pronoun-Verb-Noun) is showing exactly why word-to-word translation alone fails: languages differ in word order, so a translation system needs POS tags and structural (grammar) rules on top of a dictionary — not just substitution.

Production rules & constituency trees

S → NP VP, NP → DET? JJ? NN | PRP, VP → V NP

This is context-free grammar notation (formalized in Ch. 12 of the book): a sentence (S) rewrites into a noun phrase and verb phrase; a noun phrase is an optional determiner, optional adjective, then a noun, or a pronoun; a verb phrase is a verb followed by a noun phrase. Your tree for "I want home" (PRP → I; V → want; NP → NN → home) is one legal derivation from those rules — this structure is called a constituency tree, and the whole rule-set is called an AST-generating grammar in the parsing sense your note uses (Abstract Syntax Tree, borrowed from compiler terminology but applied to sentence structure).

The four-way task taxonomy

Your notes name four kinds of NLP tasks, then later (page 6) work through the sequence-labeling ones in detail with a real bilingual example. This is the single most useful organizing idea in the whole notebook — almost everything else in the course is an instance of one of these four.

1

Statistical LM

Next-token prediction: given a history, predict the next word. This is exactly the N-gram chapter — see N-gram language models.

2

Text/sequence classification

One label for the whole input. Example: sentiment analysis, spam detection, language identification.

3

Sequence labeling

One label per token. POS tagging is the classic case: your example tags every word of "I am going to ghar bahut dino baad" with its language (Eng/Hindi) — that's code-switching language identification done as sequence labeling.

4

Sequence generation

Output is itself a sequence, not a label. Machine translation and transliteration (हिंदी → "Namaste") are both sequence generation.

Span identification, worked

BIO tagging: (The tall kid) (is going) (to) (the school)

Your notation B-NP, I-NP, I-NP, B-VP, I-VP, B-PP, I-PP, I-PP is the standard BIO scheme (Beginning / Inside / Outside) used for chunking: B- marks the first token of a phrase (a constituent, in your word), I- marks every token continuing that same phrase. This turns "find the phrase boundaries" into a per-token classification problem — i.e., it converts span identification into sequence labeling, which is exactly why it's grouped with POS tagging in your notes.

Why tag-space explodes

|tags| = |V_E × V_H|^(sequence length)

Your page 7 derivation is about the combinatorics of sequence labeling when each position can be tagged with one of many classes (here, a cross of English-vocabulary tags and Hindi-vocabulary tags, sizes ~10,000 each). For a sequence of length n, the number of possible tag sequences is (number of tag choices)ⁿ — exponential in sentence length. That's precisely the motivation for k-beam search right below it in your notes: instead of enumerating every one of those exponentially many sequences, keep only the top-k highest-scoring partial sequences at each step and extend only those. This is a greedy/heuristic relaxation of the exact Viterbi search you'll meet again in POS tagging (Ch. 5) and in the "bag generation" exercise for N-grams.

Applications survey

Your list (code assistant, chatbot/virtual assistant, MT, ASR, TTS, summarization, speaker ID, sentiment analysis, text classification) is a menu of the four task types above, wearing different product names. Two entries are worth a slightly closer look because they're easy to mix up on an exam.

Transliteration ≠ Translation

हिंदी → "Namaste" is sound, not meaning

Transliteration rewrites the letters/sounds of a word into another script — तमस्ते → Namaste keeps the pronunciation but changes the alphabet. Nothing about meaning is involved; "computer" → कंप्यूटर (a borrowed/Romanized word written in Devanagari) is the reverse direction. Translation, by contrast, maps meaning across languages and can use entirely different words.

Language Identification (LID)

input text → which language

A single-label text classification task (one of the "four types" above). Note how it differs from the code-switch tagging example in the Tasks section: LID is document/utterance-level classification (one label for the whole input); tagging each word's language in a mixed-language sentence is token-level sequence labeling. Same underlying idea, different granularity — a distinction examiners love to test.

03/08

Tokenization

Your definition — "breaking a sentence into words, breaking a longer string into smaller units" — is exactly Ch. 3's definition (§3.9): tokenization/word segmentation is separating running text into meaningful pieces.

Granularity ladder

character → word → sentence → paragraph → document → corpus

Your arrow correctly labels the two ends: character-level is fine-grained, document/corpus-level is coarse-grained (your word "coarse-granularity"). Note: corpus is singular, corpora is plural — your correction on the page ("corpus/corpora") is catching exactly the distinction Ch. 4 relies on constantly ("the Brown corpus", "two popular corpora").

N-gram-of-length formula

#N-grams = len(unit) − N + 1

Your worked check: "You are a student." has 5 word-tokens (including the period), so for N=2: 5−2+1 = 4 bigrams. This is the same counting logic behind character N-grams: the word "boy" (3 letters) has 3−2+1 = 2 character-bigrams (bo, oy); "girl" (4 letters) has 4−2+1 = 3.

TF-IDF & count vectors

How tokens become numbers for a model

These are the two classic pre-neural ways of turning a document into a feature vector for the "convert to numeric format" pipeline step: a count vector just tallies how many times each vocabulary word appears; TF-IDF (term-frequency × inverse-document-frequency) down-weights words that appear in almost every document (like "the") and up-weights words that are frequent in this document but rare elsewhere — a first pass at separating stopwords from informative words, the same idea your Zipf's-law diagram makes visual later (see Zipf's law).

Sub-word units of speech

IPA & syllables — and why "." is hard

IPA (International Phonetic Alphabet) gives one symbol per distinct sound; a syllable is the unit of speech that can be pronounced on its own in one breath-push. Your note on punctuation tokenization is the key practical problem: a comma can pause a list, a semicolon joins two independent clauses (each with its own verb), and a period both ends a sentence and marks a decimal point or abbreviation — which is exactly why you need regex, not a fixed rule, to tell them apart (Ch. 3 §3.9 makes the same point with "$62.625" and "Mr. Sherwood").

Why grouping matters

ab+ vs. (ab)+

Exactly your example: ab+ means "a, then one-or-more b's" → ab, abb, abbb… The + binds only to the character right before it. (ab)+ means "one-or-more repetitions of the whole pair ab" → ab, abab, ababab… Parentheses force the repetition operator to apply to a group instead of a single symbol — this is the book's "operator precedence" issue (Ch. 2 §2.1.2): counters bind tighter than sequences, so without parentheses you get the wrong scope.

Escaping special characters

\+ to match a literal "+"

Since +, *, . etc. are operators, matching the literal character requires a backslash: \+ matches "+" itself, \* matches "*". Your greedy-vs-non-greedy example nails the key gotcha: ^.+.$ is greedy — it expands as far as possible, so on "I am at SVNIT, SURAT. I have taken the boring NLP class." it will match from the first character all the way to the last period, swallowing both sentences, because . matches anything including other periods. A non-greedy quantifier (commonly written +?) stops at the first match instead.

Character classes

\d \D \w \W \s \S

\d=[0-9], \D=non-digit, \w=alphanumeric+underscore, \W=non-alphanumeric, \s=whitespace, \S=non-whitespace. Note \S+ ≠ \w+: \S also matches punctuation, \w doesn't.

Anchors, refined

^[a-z] vs [^a-z]

Caret outside brackets at the start of a pattern = "starts with"; caret as the first character inside brackets = negation ("does not contain"). Same symbol, opposite jobs depending on position — a classic trick question.

Unicode block

Devanagari & ASCII

ASCII covers 256 characters — not enough for Devanagari (Hindi's script), which needs its own Unicode block. Your example "हिंदी → only captures ह" if you forget \w+/the Devanagari range, because "ी" (a vowel-sign / matra) isn't in the plain alphanumeric character class by default — you must explicitly include the Unicode range.

Search vs. match

Both return an index — but scope differs

re.match(pattern, x) only checks the pattern against the start of the string; re.search(pattern, x) scans the whole string for the first occurrence anywhere. Your example: for x="abcd", re.match('b+', x) fails (x doesn't start with b), but re.search('b+', x) succeeds.

findall & groups

Extracting an email's parts

Your pattern ^(\w+)@(\w+)\.(\w+)$ against "abc@example.com" — findall returns a list of tuples, one per match, each tuple holding the captured groups: [('abc','example','com')]. Each parenthesized group in the pattern becomes one slot in the tuple.

Substitution needs a function, not another regex

Why sub('(am|are)','were',x) is wrong for context-sensitive replacement

Your worked example is a great catch: to turn "I am going" into "we were going" and "you are going" into "we were going", the pattern to search for can be one regex, (am|are) — but the replacement can't be a single fixed string, since both need to become "were" while other am/are's elsewhere might need different treatment. Python's re.sub solves this by accepting a callback function instead of a literal string:

def repl(match_object):
  if match_object.group(1) == 'am': return 'was'
  elif match_object.group(1) == 'are': return 'were'
  else: return None

re.sub('(am|are)', repl, x)

This exact mechanism — pattern match, then a per-match decision — is what powers ELIZA's cascade of substitutions in Ch. 2 §2.1.6.

Annotation formats: why we bracket, and how corpora get stored

Why brackets/tags at all

Force precedence → readability → eliminate ambiguity

Your three-word chain is the whole justification: an annotation scheme (like POS tags or XML-style brackets) exists purely to remove ambiguity about what modifies what, and to make a corpus machine-readable in a structured, unambiguous way.

SSF (Shakti Standard Format)

<sentence id='1'> ... </sentence>, tab-separated

Each token gets a line number, the token itself, and its POS tag, tab-delimited, inside an SSF/CoNLL-style block:

2  am   VAUX
3  going VM
4  home  NN

CoNLL format

One token per line, blank line = sentence boundary

CoNLL drops the explicit XML tags and index numbers of SSF; a blank line alone signals where one sentence ends and the next begins. It's the format almost every modern POS/NER dataset ships in, because it's trivial to stream line-by-line.

M = (Q, Σ, δ, q₀, F) — exactly your notation: Q states, Σ input alphabet, δ the transition function, q₀ the start state, F the set of final/accepting states. State q3 has a self-loop on "a" (any number of extra a's), and only q4 (double circle) is accepting.

Deterministic vs. non-deterministic

Same language, different transition tables

A DFA has at most one legal transition per (state, symbol) pair — the machine never has to guess. An NFA can have several transitions for the same (state, symbol) pair (or ε-transitions that move without consuming input), so recognizing a string may require trying multiple paths — exactly the "agenda" search (LIFO/depth-first or FIFO/breadth-first) the book's ND-RECOGNIZE algorithm implements. Every NFA has an equivalent DFA (possibly with exponentially more states).

D-RECOGNIZE, in words

Walk the tape, follow the transition table, or reject

Start at state q₀ and the first tape cell. Loop: if input is exhausted, accept iff current state is final; else look up (current-state, current-symbol) in the transition table — if that cell is empty, reject; otherwise move to the new state and advance the tape pointer by one. This is a linear-time algorithm, and it's the "correct word identifier" your page 22 note describes: FSA-based dictionary lookup accepts exactly the strings that are legal words.

Fail/sink state

Every "empty" table cell is really an arc to a trap state

Conceptually, any transition-table gap points to an implicit fail state q_fail that swallows the rest of the input and never accepts — this is what your page 21 arrows converging on a single node ("q_fail") for every illegal (state, input) pair are drawing.

Morphology & finite-state transducers

This is Chapter 3 in full: parsing (definition), morphological analysis vs. generation, the ASR↔TTS cycle, inflectional vs. derivational morphology, and finally FSTs with the fox→foxes worked example.

Textwritten form
⇄
Speechaudio signal

TTS goes text→speech; ASR goes speech→text. Your diagram's cycle is exactly this pair of inverse systems.

Parsing (general sense)

Extracting/providing grammatical information about a word

Morphological analysis: cats = cat + N + PL (surface form → stem + features). Morph generation is the reverse: cat + N + PL → cats. They are inverse operations — and because an FST maps between two tapes, the same transducer run forward does generation and run backward (inverted) does analysis. This is the book's "FST as translator" idea (§3.4), and it's why your notes list analysis and generation as "reverse of each other".

Inflectional vs. derivational

Category stays the same vs. category can change

Inflectional: walk → walks/walking/walked — always still a verb, meaning doesn't fundamentally change, just grammatical features (tense, person, number) do. Derivational: nation (N) → national (Adj) → nationalize (V) → nationalization (N) — each step can change the part of speech, and the meaning shift is less predictable. Your "nation→national→nationalize→nationalization" chain is the textbook's exact nominalization example pattern (§3.1.2) run through several suffixes: -al, -ize, -ation.

Morpheme, root, affix

cats = cat (stem/root) + -s (suffix, inflection)

A morpheme is the smallest meaning-bearing unit. unhappy = un- (prefix, derivation) + happy (root). Your rope example is a nice edge case: "rope" itself is a free root that can be a noun or a verb, and "roped" (rope + -ed) shows a regular past-tense inflection stacks on top of either reading.

Free vs. bound morphemes

cat can stand alone; -s cannot

A free morpheme (cat) is a word by itself. A bound morpheme (-s, un-, -tion) never occurs alone — "it carries no meaning on its own, only grammatical information," exactly as your note states.

The FST core idea

Two tapes, not one — input maps to output

FSA = recognizer  →  one tape, accepts a language
FST = transducer  →  two tapes (input, output), defines a relation between two languages

Your four uses of an FST match Ch. 3 §3.4 exactly: recognition (accept/reject a pair of strings), generation (produce output pairs), translation (read one string, output another), relating sets. And the two closure properties that make FSTs practical: inversion (swap input/output tapes — turns a parser into a generator for free) and composition (chain two transducers into one, T₁∘T₂).

Worked: cats and fox→foxes, with the intermediate tape

Why you need a THIRD tape, not just lexical and surface

Your three tapes — surface (cats), intermediate (cat^s#), lexical (cat+N+PL) — are exactly Fig. 3.12–3.16 of the book. The lexicon transducer maps lexical ⇄ intermediate (concatenating morphemes with boundary markers ^ and #); a separate orthographic-rule transducer maps intermediate ⇄ surface, handling spelling changes.

Lexical:     fox +N +Pl
Intermediate: fox ^ s #
Surface:    fox e s  ← e-insertion rule fires here

The e-insertion rule: insert e on the surface tape whenever the lexical tape ends a morpheme in x/s/z and the next morpheme is the plural -s. Without it, "fox+s" would surface as the illegal "foxs". "cats", by contrast, needs no insertion (cat ends in t), so the intermediate and surface tapes coincide once the boundary markers are stripped.

Your die-roll worked example

P(X=1,Y=3) = 1/36 — confirms joint = count / total

For two independent die rolls, only one of the 36 equally-likely (X,Y) outcomes has X=1,Y=3, so P(X=1,Y=3)=1/36 exactly matches your formula P(X=x,Y=y)=C(X=x,Y=y)/ΣC(X=x′,Y=y′). Then marginalization (page 31) — summing a joint distribution over one variable to recover the other's marginal — gives P(X=1)=Σ_y P(X=1,Y=y)=6×(1/36)=1/6, exactly the unconditional probability of rolling a 1. This is the probability-theory groundwork the whole N-gram chapter is built on.

<s>/</s> padding rule

For an N-gram model, pad with (N−1) BOS and (N−1) EOS

Bigram: one <s> before, one </s> after. Trigram: two <s>'s before, two </s>'s after — so P(w₁|<s><s>) is defined for the very first word. This matches the book's footnote in §4.2 exactly: without a symmetric end token, sentence probabilities of a given length would sum to 1 and the whole language's probability would be infinite.

MLE = relative frequency

P(wᵢ | wᵢ₋₂,wᵢ₋₁) = C(wᵢ₋₂ wᵢ₋₁ wᵢ) / C(wᵢ₋₂ wᵢ₋₁)

Your page 33 "max P(wᵢ|w₁…w_{i-1})" framing — picking the highest-probability next word at each step to build "The → car → …" — is describing greedy decoding: generate text by always taking the argmax next word under the N-gram model. It's cheap but not optimal (true optimal-sequence search needs Viterbi, same idea as k-beam search from the Tasks section).

The motivating zero-count example

"The book is on the table" — but C(on the table) = 0

This is the whole reason smoothing exists (book §4.5, opening paragraph): even a perfectly grammatical, common-sounding trigram can have zero count in a finite training corpus, which would zero out the probability of an entire sentence under raw MLE. Every technique in the next section is a different answer to "what do we do about that zero?"

24/08 – 01/09

The smoothing zoo

Your notes cover, in order: Add-1, Add-k (with grid search), discounting, Witten-Bell, Good-Turing (fully worked with real numbers!), Katz backoff, absolute discounting, and Kneser-Ney. Each is a refinement of the one before — read them in this order and the whole family clicks into place.

Add-1 (Laplace)

P(wᵢ|wᵢ₋₁) = [C(wᵢ₋₁wᵢ)+1] / [C(wᵢ₋₁)+V]

Add one to every count so nothing is ever exactly zero; compensate the denominator by +V (vocabulary size) so probabilities still sum to 1. Your worked number: C(want→to)=608 out of C(want)=1,000,000 gives raw MLE P=0.0003; with add-1 and V≈100,000, it becomes 609/1,000,100 ≈ 0.0000309969 ≈ 0.000031 = 3.1×10⁻⁵ — barely moved, because V is tiny relative to a huge corpus here. (With a small corpus, as in the textbook's Berkeley Restaurant example, add-1 moves probabilities drastically — see the Field Guide's smoothing section for that contrast.)

Add-k, tuned by grid search

P(wᵢ|wᵢ₋₁) = [C(wᵢ₋₁wᵢ)+k] / [C(wᵢ₋₁)+kV], 0<k<1

Add-1 over-corrects when V is large; add a smaller fraction k instead. Your recipe for choosing k is textbook hyperparameter search: sweep kᵢ = kᵢ₋₁ + 0.05 over a grid (not exhaustive, just a fixed step size), evaluate each candidate's smoothed probabilities against the un-smoothed ones or against held-out likelihood, and keep whichever k gives the best score (smallest gap/discrepancy, or highest held-out probability).

Discounting — the unifying idea

c′(wᵢ₋₁,wᵢ) = [C(wᵢ₋₁,wᵢ)+1]/[C(wᵢ₋₁)+V×1] × C(wᵢ₋₁,wᵢ)

Every smoothing method can be described as computing an adjusted count c* that's a little smaller than the raw MLE count, freeing up probability mass to give to unseen events. The discount ratio d = c*/c tells you how much of the original count survived. This single idea — steal a little mass from what you've seen, give it to what you haven't — is the throughline connecting every method below.

Witten-Bell — the formal (Chen & Goodman) interpolated definition

P_WB(wᵢ|h) = τ(wᵢ|h) + γ(h)·P_WB(wᵢ|h′)

h is the history (context); h′ is that same history with its oldest word dropped — one order shorter. N₁₊(h•) ("N-one-plus") is the number of distinct word types that have ever followed h at least once — this is the γ term's whole job: a context that's historically been followed by many different words is "more open," so more of its probability mass gets reserved for backing off.

N₁₊(h•) = |{wᵢ : C(h,wᵢ) > 0}|  ← # distinct continuations ever seen after h

γ(h) = N₁₊(h•) / [N₁₊(h•) + C(h)]  ← mass handed down to the shorter history h′
τ(wᵢ|h) = C(h,wᵢ) / [N₁₊(h•) + C(h)]  ← mass kept from this context's own relative frequency

Check: τ(wᵢ|h) summed over all wᵢ, plus γ(h), always equals exactly 1 — so this is a genuine probability distribution, not just a heuristic patch. Substituting γ and τ back in and combining over a common denominator gives the single-line form most courses quote:

P_WB(wᵢ|h) = [C(h,wᵢ) + N₁₊(h•)·P_WB(wᵢ|h′)] / [C(h) + N₁₊(h•)]
Worked example — bigram P_WB(is|learning) on the "ML corpus"

Formal answer: 0.595  vs.  your notes' shortcut "+1" version: 0.714

Reusing the corpus from the N-gram section below: C(learning)=5, C(learning,is)=4, N₁₊(learning•)=2 (learning is followed by only is and can). For the recursive base case, take P_WB(is) ≈ its plain unigram MLE, C(is)/N = 4/48 (a common simplification — the "true" base case recurses one level further, to a uniform 1/V zerogram, which most courses skip for hand computation).

τ(is|learning) = C(learning,is) / [N₁₊(learning•)+C(learning)] = 4/(2+5) = 4/7 ≈ 0.5714
γ(learning) = N₁₊(learning•) / [N₁₊(learning•)+C(learning)] = 2/7 ≈ 0.2857
P_WB(is) ≈ C(is)/N = 4/48 ≈ 0.0833

P_WB(is|learning) = 0.5714 + 0.2857 × 0.0833 = 0.5952

Your class notes' page-38 shortcut — P(wᵢ|h) = [C(h,wᵢ)+1] / [C(h)+N₁₊(h•)] — plugs in the same numbers as (4+1)/(5+2) = 5/7 ≈ 0.7143, a visibly different number. That shortcut swaps the recursive γ(h)·P_WB(wᵢ|h′) term for a flat "+1," which makes it fast to hand-compute but is not the textbook-accurate Witten-Bell formula — treat it as a simplified teaching approximation, and use the τ/γ version above if a question asks for "Witten-Bell smoothing" without further qualification.

Good-Turing — your worked numeric example, reproduced exactly

Frequency of frequencies: x,y,z (count 1), a,b (count 2), c (count 3)

N₁ = 3

items seen exactly once: x, y, z

N₂ = 2

items seen exactly twice: a, b

N₃ = 1

items seen exactly 3×: c

Total count N = 1×N₁ + 2×N₂ + 3×N₃ = 3 + 4 + 3 = 10

c*₁ = (1+1)·N₂/N₁ = (2)(2)/3 = 4/3 ≈ 1.33  ← discounted count for a singleton
c*₂ = (2+1)·N₃/N₂ = (3)(1)/2 = 3/2 = 1.5  ← discounted count for a doubleton

Total probability mass reserved for unseen = N₁/N = 3/10 = 0.3
With 2 unseen items {u, v}: P*GT(unseen) = 0.3 / 2 = 0.15 each

This is the exact mechanism as the book's fish example (bass/catfish unseen, trout/salmon/eel seen once) — same formula, different letters. Two practical snags your notes raise and answer:

  • When N_{c+1}=0 (a gap in the frequency table): either average — replace N_c with N_c ÷ ((next-higher-populated-bin − previous-lower-populated-bin)/2) — or use the "hack" of substituting the nearest higher non-zero N_c in the ratio. The book's more rigorous version of this same fix is Simple Good-Turing: fit log(N_c) = a + b·log(c) by linear regression (since it's linear in log-log space) and read smoothed N_c values off that line instead of the noisy raw counts.
  • Large counts stay untouched: above a threshold (Katz suggests k=5), just use c*=c — the empirical count is already trustworthy, and applying discounting to every count would waste effort.
Katz backoff

Uses a Good-Turing discount d = c*/c, plus a normalizing factor

P_Katz(wᵢ|wᵢ₋₁) = P*(wᵢ|wᵢ₋₁) if the bigram was seen; otherwise α(wᵢ₋₁) × P*(wᵢ) — back off to the unigram, scaled by a normalizer α so the whole distribution still sums to 1. The discounted numerator P* always undercuts raw MLE (P* < P_MLE), which is exactly what frees up the mass α redistributes downward.

Absolute discounting

Subtract a flat constant d (often 0.75 for English), not a ratio

P_AD(wᵢ|wᵢ₋₁) = max(C(wᵢ₋₁wᵢ)−d, 0)/C(wᵢ₋₁) + λ(wᵢ₋₁)·P_AD(lower order). The book's own empirical observation (§4.9.1) is exactly what motivates this: once you tabulate Good-Turing's c* against raw c, the adjusted counts for c≥2 all sit suspiciously close to "raw count minus about 0.75" — so why not just subtract that constant directly and skip re-estimating it per count? Simpler, and nearly as good.

Stupid backoff

No real probabilities at all — just a fixed discount, for speed at web scale

P_bo(wᵢ|wᵢ₋₁) = P_MLE(wᵢ|wᵢ₋₁) if C(wᵢ₋₁wᵢ)>0, else 0.4 × P_MLE(wᵢ)

The constant 0.4 is arbitrary and the result isn't a true probability distribution (it doesn't sum to 1) — but it's cheap to compute over billions of N-grams, which is the entire point: Google's web-scale N-gram release (mentioned in the book's bibliographical notes, §4.11) needed something this fast.

Kneser-Ney smoothing — the modern standard

Your reading-glasses example is the book's San Francisco example, inverted

"When I am reading a novel for a few hours, I need my reading ___" → glasses, not some rarer-but-frequent word. The book's version: Francisco is a frequent word overall, but it's frequent only because it almost always follows San — so backing off to raw unigram frequency would wrongly favor Francisco in a new context. Glasses appears after many different words, so it's the more versatile — more continuation-worthy — choice.

P_CONT(wᵢ) = |{w′ : C(w′,wᵢ)>0}| / |{(w′,w″) : C(w′,w″)>0}|
= (# distinct words that precede wᵢ) / (# distinct bigram types in the whole corpus)
Bigram Kneser-Ney:
P_KN(wᵢ|wᵢ₋₁) = max(C(wᵢ₋₁,wᵢ)−d, 0)/ΣC(wᵢ₋₁,w′)  +  λ(wᵢ₋₁)·P_CONT(wᵢ)

λ(wᵢ₋₁) = d × |{w′: C(wᵢ₋₁,w′)>0}| / ΣC(wᵢ₋₁,w′)  ← leftover mass, redistributed via P_CONT

λ(wᵢ₋₁) is exactly "how many distinct words followed this context, times the discount, normalized" — the same intuition as Witten-Bell's N(context) term, reused to compute how much mass to hand down to the continuation-probability backoff. For a trigram, the same shape recurses one level deeper: P_KN(wᵢ|wᵢ₋₂wᵢ₋₁) backs off to P_KN(wᵢ|wᵢ₋₁), which in turn backs off to the unigram P_CONT.

Interpolation vs. backoff

Interpolation: always blend every order

P = λ₁P(wᵢ|wᵢ₋₂wᵢ₋₁) + λ₂P(wᵢ|wᵢ₋₁) + λ₃P(wᵢ), Σλ=1

Your "point between P and Q" analogy is a genuinely good one: just as a weighted midpoint (mx₁+nx₂)/(m+n) sits between two points depending on the weights m,n, interpolation always mixes trigram, bigram, and unigram estimates together — never relying on just one order alone, even when the higher-order count is healthy. Constraint: λ₁+λ₂+λ₃=1, and typically λ₁>λ₂>λ₃ (trust the most specific, highest-order estimate the most, when it's available).

Backoff: use ONE order, chosen by availability

Trigram if seen; else fall to bigram; else unigram

The key exam distinction: interpolation is a weighted sum of all orders every time; backoff is a conditional cascade — you only ever consult a lower order when the higher one has zero count. Both need the λ or α weights tuned via grid search on a held-out development set, never on the final test set (same train/dev/test discipline from the Pipeline section).

Zipf's Law

rank ∝ 1/frequency

freq = a·b⁻ʳ, or log(freq) = log(a) − r·log(b)

Plotting word frequency against rank gives a steep curve that's actually a straight line once you take logs — an inverse power law. The practical payoff, drawn right on your curve: the head of the distribution (rank 1, 2, 3…) is dominated by stopwords (the, a, is…) that carry almost no topical meaning; the long tail is where the informative content words live, but each individually has a very low count — which is precisely the sparse-data problem the entire smoothing chapter exists to solve. Fitting the line (a,b) is a small regression problem: minimize Σ(cᵢ − c_pred,ᵢ)² over the observed rank/frequency pairs — ordinary least squares, same idea as any linear-regression fit.

Byte-Pair Encoding (BPE)

Originally a data-compression algorithm (Gage, 1994), repurposed as a subword tokenizer — the technique that actually sits underneath your "pretrained tokenizer" note from Day 2.

Your worked corpus

huggingface(5), hugs(6), bugs(7), bun(4), bag(3), hugging(2)

Starting vocabulary V_init = every unique character: {h,u,g,i,n,f,a,c,e,s,b}. BPE repeatedly counts adjacent symbol-pairs across the whole (frequency-weighted) corpus and merges the single most frequent pair into a new symbol, adding it to the vocabulary — then repeats on the updated corpus.

Round 1 — pair counts include: count(h,u)=13, count(u,g)=20  ← most frequent
Merge (u,g) → "ug".  New vocabulary V₁ = V_init ∪ {ug}

Round 2 — recount pairs on the corpus written with "ug" as one symbol.
Most frequent now: (h, ug) → merge into "hug".  V₂ = V₁ ∪ {hug}

… repeat …

Stopping rule, exactly as your note states: stop merging once the vocabulary stops changing (or once you hit a pre-set target vocabulary size). The payoff: any new word at inference time — even one never seen in training — can still be represented as a sequence of known subword pieces instead of a single out-of-vocabulary token, which is the single biggest practical limitation BPE fixes relative to whole-word tokenization (and, by extension, relative to plain word-level N-gram models, which have no answer at all for a word outside their fixed vocabulary V).

WordPiece

Google's variant

Merges the pair that most increases the training-data likelihood under a unigram language model, rather than simply the most frequent pair — a small but real refinement over vanilla BPE.

SentencePiece

Language-agnostic variant

Treats the input as a raw stream of Unicode characters (including whitespace as a normal symbol) rather than assuming whitespace already separates words — useful for languages like Chinese/Japanese/Thai, which your Ch. 3 notes on Chinese segmentation (maximum matching) already flagged as not using whitespace at all.
