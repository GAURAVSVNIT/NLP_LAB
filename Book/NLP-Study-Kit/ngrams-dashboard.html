§4.0 Motivation

Why predict the next word?

Anywhere language arrives noisy or ambiguous, a model of "what word is likely here" disambiguates it. Four tasks from the chapter, each solved the same way — by preferring the higher-probability word sequence.

Speech recognition

"I have a gun" vs. "I have a gub"

A sloppy hold-up note reads as a non-word. An N-gram grammar knows gun is astronomically more probable after "I have a" than gub or gull.

Machine translation

briefed reporters > briefed to reporters

Several rough translations are all grammatical. The one with the highest N-gram probability is chosen as the most fluent.

Spelling correction

"fifteen minuets" → "fifteen minutes"

minuets is a real word, so a dictionary lookup won't catch it. in about fifteen minutes simply has a far higher sequence probability.

Augmentative comm.

Word-menu prediction

For users who select words via limited body movement (as Stephen Hawking did), N-grams rank the menu so the likely next word is one click away.

§4.2 From chain rule to N-gram

The three-step derivation examiners love to ask for

You cannot count long histories — they never repeat. The chain rule is exact but useless; the Markov assumption trades exactness for something countable.

1 · Chain rule (exact)P(w₁…wₙ) = ∏ₖ P(wₖ | w₁…wₖ₋₁)
→
2 · Markov assumptionP(wₙ | w₁…wₙ₋₁) ≈ P(wₙ | wₙ₋ₙ₊₁…wₙ₋₁)
→
3 · Bigram case (N=2)P(wₙ | w₁…wₙ₋₁) ≈ P(wₙ | wₙ₋₁)
Definition

Maximum Likelihood Estimate (MLE)

Count the bigram, normalize by the count of its prefix — the unigram count of the first word.

P(wₙ | wₙ₋₁) = C(wₙ₋₁ wₙ) / C(wₙ₋₁)
General case

N-gram MLE (Eq. 4.15)

Same idea, generalized: count the N-gram, divide by the count of the (N−1)-word prefix.

P(wₙ | wⁿ⁻¹ₙ₋ₙ₊₁) = C(wⁿₙ₋ₙ₊₁) / C(wⁿ⁻¹ₙ₋ₙ₊₁)

MLE is "the estimate that makes the observed training data most probable." C(Chinese)=400 in a 1M-word corpus ⇒ P(Chinese)=.0004 — the value most likely to have produced exactly 400 occurrences.

Chaining it into a sentence

P(<s> i want chinese food </s>)

= P(i|<s>) × P(want|i) × P(chinese|want) × P(food|chinese) × P(</s>|food)
= 0.25 × 0.33 × 0.0065 × 0.52 × 0.68

§4.3 Training and test sets

Splits, held-out data, and words you've never seen

An N-gram model can only be as honest as its evaluation. Never let test sentences leak into training — that's "training on the test set," and it makes perplexity look artificially good.

Typical split

80 / 10 / 10

Training set (fit N-gram counts) · development/test set (tune hyperparameters like λ or k) · final test set (report once, untouched until then).

Held-out set

Not for counting — for tuning

Used to learn interpolation weights λᵢ, e.g. by maximizing held-out likelihood, often via EM.

Open vs. closed vocabulary

<UNK> handles the unknown

Fix a vocabulary in advance, map every out-of-training word to <UNK> during normalization, then estimate <UNK>'s probability like any other token.

N-gram sensitivity to genre — Shakespeare vs. Wall Street Journal

Both trigram grammars produce "English-like" sentences, but they share almost no phrases. Lesson: train on data that resembles your test domain.

Shakespeare trigram:
"Sweet prince, Falstaff shall die. Harry of Monmouth's grave."
WSJ trigram:
"They also point to ninety nine point six billion dollars…"
§4.4 Evaluating N-grams

Perplexity: the inverse probability, normalized

Lower perplexity ⇒ the model was less surprised by the test set ⇒ better model. It also reads as the weighted average branching factor — how many words could plausibly come next.

PP(W) = P(w₁w₂…w_N)−1/N  =  ᴺ√( 1 / P(w₁w₂…w_N) )  =  ᴺ√( ∏ᵢ 1 / P(wᵢ|wᵢ₋₁) ) bigram case

Sanity check

10 equiprobable digits ⇒ PP = 10

Each of 0–9 has P=.1. Perplexity of a random digit string equals exactly the branching factor: 10.

Skew lowers perplexity

Same 10 digits, "0" ×10 more frequent ⇒ PP ≈ 5.66

Branching factor is still 10 candidates, but the effective uncertainty drops because one outcome dominates. Full derivation in the Numericals pack.

§4.5–4.7 Smoothing

Rescuing probability mass from the zeros

Any finite corpus has unseen N-grams that are perfectly good English. MLE gives them probability zero — which zeroes out an entire test-sentence probability. Smoothing steals a little mass from the seen events and gives it to the unseen ones.

Laplace / add-one

Add 1 to every count

P*(wₙ|wₙ₋₁) = [C(wₙ₋₁wₙ)+1] / [C(wₙ₋₁)+V]

Simple, but moves far too much mass when V is large. Never used in modern systems — pedagogical baseline only.

Good-Turing

Re-estimate c from "c+1"-frequency

c* = (c+1) · Nc+1 / Nc
P*(unseen) = N₁ / N

Uses the count of singletons (hapax legomena) to estimate the mass owed to things never seen at all.

Kneser-Ney

Backoff by versatility, not frequency

P_CONT(w) ∝ |{wᵢ₋₁ : C(wᵢ₋₁w)>0}|

Francisco is frequent but almost always after San; glasses appears after many different words. Kneser-Ney backs off to "how many contexts", not "how often" — today's standard method.

§4.6–4.7 Combining orders

Backoff vs. interpolation

Both fall back on lower-order N-grams when data is sparse — but they disagree about when to consult them.

Backoff (Katz)

Trigram count > 0 ?
↓ yes
Use discounted trigram P*
↓ no → recurse
Back off to bigram, then unigram

Only one order is ever used per prediction. Requires discounting (P* / α) — otherwise probabilities sum to more than 1.

Interpolation

λ₁·P(trigram)
+
λ₂·P(bigram)
+
λ₃·P(unigram)

All three orders are always mixed, weighted by λᵢ (Σλᵢ=1), tuned on held-out data. Interpolated Kneser-Ney > its own backoff version.

§4.8–4.9 Practical & advanced

ARPA format, classes, cache, and the web as a corpus

Storage

ARPA format — log₁₀ probabilities + backoff weight α

\1-grams:\
-1.776296  want  -1.04292
\2-grams:\
-0.4861297  i want  0.0425899
\3-grams:\
-0.4120701  to go to

Log space avoids underflow from multiplying many small probabilities, and turns multiplication into (cheaper) addition.

Toolkits

SRILM & the Cambridge-CMU toolkit

Raw text in (order N, discounting method, thresholds) → ARPA-format model out. Both compute perplexity/decoding from that same file.

Class-based N-grams

P(wᵢ|wᵢ₋₁) ≈ P(cᵢ|cᵢ₋₁)·P(wᵢ|cᵢ)

IBM clustering: hard-assign each word to one class (CITYNAME, MONTH…), fixing sparsity for "to Shanghai" if "to London/Beijing/Denver" were already seen.

Cache models

A word just used will likely recur

Mix a unigram model built from the preceding part of the same document with the static N-gram. Fails when earlier words were themselves errors (locks in mistakes).

Web-scale counts

p̂_web = c_web(w₁w₂w₃) / c_web(w₁w₂)

Search-engine page counts approximate true web N-gram counts and can be mixed into a conventional model — noisy, but useful.

§4.10 Information-theory background

Entropy, cross-entropy, and where perplexity comes from

Perplexity is literally 2^H. Entropy H(X) = −Σ p(x)·log₂p(x) is the minimum average bits needed to encode outcomes drawn from p.

Cover & Thomas' horse-race example

8 horses, skewed odds → H(X) = 2 bits (not 3)

Horse 1 wins half the time; horses 5–8 are longshots at 1/64 each. A naive fixed-length code needs 3 bits/horse; an optimal code needs only the entropy: 2 bits on average.

Horse 1 · ½
H2 · ¼
H3

Segment width = probability mass. This is exactly the Shannon technique used to generate random N-gram sentences: lay words on [0,1] by frequency, throw a dart.

Cross-entropy

H(p,m) ≥ H(p), always

m is your model, p is the true (unknown) distribution. The closer your model, the tighter this upper bound — so lower cross-entropy (= lower perplexity) is strictly better.

English's entropy

~1.3 bits/letter (Shannon) · 1.75 bits/char (Brown et al. trigram)

At 5.5 letters/word average, that implies a per-word perplexity around 142 for general English — far higher than the WSJ-on-WSJ number of 109, because train/test genre match matters.
